# Human Voice

Writing generated by LLMs converges on a recognizable style — not because the ideas are bad, but because the model defaults to safe, inflated, hedge-everything prose. Readers now pattern-match this instantly and it kills trust, especially on Substack where the whole pitch is "a person is telling you something real." This skill is a checklist to run against a draft (yours or the user's) before calling it done, sourced from [Wikipedia's Signs of AI Writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) catalog, adapted for essay/newsletter writing rather than encyclopedia editing.

## Why this matters here

A Substack post lives or dies on voice. A reader who smells AI tone stops trusting the argument, not just the prose. The fixes below aren't style nitpicks — each one is a spot where the model reached for a safe generic pattern instead of committing to a specific, checkable claim. Fixing the tell almost always fixes an underlying vagueness too.

## The pass: run this after drafting, before calling a post done

Read the draft once for content, then once *only* for these patterns. Don't fix as you read the first time — you'll miss things by getting pulled into rewriting mid-scan.

### 1. Inflated significance
Watch for: "pivotal moment," "marks a shift," "underscores the importance of," "a testament to." These claim significance instead of demonstrating it.
Fix: cut the claim, or replace with the concrete fact that would make a reader conclude it themselves. If you can delete the sentence and lose no information, delete it.

### 2. AI-vocabulary tics
Watch for: delve, underscore, pivotal, intricate, testament, landscape, tapestry, crucial, enduring, fostering, leverage, robust, seamless, "in today's world," "in the realm of."
Fix: swap for the plain word you'd actually say out loud to a friend. If no plain word exists, the sentence is probably saying nothing — cut it.

### 3. Copula avoidance
Watch for: "serves as," "functions as," "stands as," "represents" where "is" would do.
Fix: use "is." It's not weaker, it's just direct — direct reads as confident, not lazy.

### 4. Negative parallelisms and rule-of-three
Watch for: "not just X, but Y," "it's not about X, it's about Y," and any list of exactly three adjectives or exactly three examples in a row ("faster, cheaper, and more reliable").
Fix: these constructions manufacture a contrast or a sense of completeness that isn't earned. State the one thing you actually mean. If a real list has 2, 4, or 5 items, use that number, not 3.

### 5. Vague attribution
Watch for: "researchers argue," "many believe," "critics say," "it's often noted" — with no name attached.
Fix: name the person, source, or your own claim. "I think" or "In my experience" beats a floating unattributed authority every time — it's also more honest about what you actually know.

### 6. Promotional / travel-brochure adjectives
Watch for: vibrant, nestled, showcasing, game-changing, cutting-edge, unlock, elevate.
Fix: describe the specific thing that makes it good instead of asserting that it's good.

### 7. Structural boilerplate
Watch for a rigid "despite challenges, the future looks promising" close, or a formulaic intro that restates the title as a definition ("X refers to...").
Fix: end on the actual conclusion or open question you have, not a hedge-everything gesture at optimism. Start with a hook, a scene, or a claim — not a dictionary definition of your own topic.

### 8. Formatting tells
Watch for: excessive bolding of key terms, em-dashes used as a tic (more than ~1 per few paragraphs), curly quotes/apostrophes if the rest of the piece uses straight ones, title-case headers, emoji used as bullet markers.
Fix: bold only what you'd actually emphasize speaking aloud. Vary sentence connectors instead of chaining em-dashes. Keep quote style consistent with your own typing habits.

### 9. Forced lexical variety
Watch for: a single concept getting a different synonym every time it's mentioned ("the company... the firm... the organization... the enterprise...") because the model is avoiding repetition.
Fix: repeat the plain word. Humans repeat words; only models are penalized for it. Repetition here reads as clarity, not clumsiness.

## Applying this while drafting, not just reviewing

If you're generating the post yourself (not just reviewing a human's draft), don't write it AI-flavored and then clean it up — that's more work and the tics leak through anyway. Instead:
- Commit to specific claims and named sources as you go, not vague ones you plan to fix later.
- Write like you're telling a specific person the point over coffee, not summarizing a topic for a general audience.
- Say the actual number, name, or example instead of the category it belongs to.
- End sections when the point is made — don't add a wrap-up sentence restating what was just said.

## What this skill does NOT do

It doesn't guarantee "undetectable" writing, and that's not the goal — the goal is a post that sounds like the person who's supposedly writing it, not text engineered to fool a detector. Per the source article: no single tell proves AI origin, and some of these patterns show up in real human writing too (some writers genuinely love em-dashes). Use judgment — if the user's own established voice already does one of these things intentionally, don't sand it off.

## Quick self-check before shipping

Read the piece aloud in your head as if talking to one specific real person. Any sentence that only makes sense in "essay voice" and not "talking voice" is a candidate for a cut or rewrite.
